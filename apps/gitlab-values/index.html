§ 00 / FoundationThe big idea

CREDIT isn't six unrelated ideals. It's an interlocking system that produces a particular way of working — and one that GitLab uses as the explicit basis for hiring decisions.

The shorthand: CREDIT = the credit team members give each other by assuming positive intent. Values are interlinked and frequently in tension — almost every hard decision at GitLab is one value pulling against another (e.g. transparency vs. efficiency, results vs. iteration). Resolution comes from conversation, not a hierarchy of values.

"Almost every time we face a hard decision at GitLab, it's because values are in conflict. It's not binary logic. It requires conversation, and sometimes there is no obvious answer." — Sid Sijbrandij, GitLab co-founder & CEO

Three things that change how you read everything below

1. Values are a hiring filter, not posters

GitLab explicitly hires and rewards on values — "culture fit is a bad excuse". They want cultural diversity over cultural conformity, but expect alignment with the six values themselves. Expect direct values-fit questions and probing for evidence in your STAR stories.

2. Values have sub-values

Each of the six has multiple sub-values. The "sub" stands for substantiate, not subordinate — they are the actionable, behavioural specifics. The most-cited cross-cutting sub-values (kindness, short toes, assume positive intent, no ego, manager of one, MVC, dogfooding) carry as much weight in interviews as the headline values.

3. The handbook is the source of truth

Handbook-first means: if a process isn't in the handbook, it doesn't exist. Decisions made in Slack or meetings only become real when committed to the handbook. This mindset shows up everywhere — in async work, in transparency, in how onboarding works.

4. The values are a living document

The values page is iterated like any other handbook page — anyone can propose changes via merge request. GitLab has noted that they previously had more values than they do now and condensed them into CREDIT with sub-values because the longer list wasn't memorable enough. The values themselves continue to be refined, which is consistent with iteration as a value.

What's verified vs. what to double-check before the interview

Solid: the six CREDIT values, the headline cultural sub-values (kindness, no ego, short toes, assume positive intent, manager of one, MVC, dogfooding, steel-manning, disagree-commit-disagree, "faintest pencil > sharpest memory", "wrong solutions are fixable"), the existence of SAFE and Confidentiality Levels frameworks, handbook-first, async-first, low-context communication, public-by-default. § 01 / The C in CREDIT🤝Collaboration

"Helping others is a priority, even when it is not immediately related to the goals that you are trying to achieve."

Anyone can chime in on any subject — including people who don't work at GitLab. The DRI (Directly Responsible Individual) decides how to do the work, but takes every suggestion seriously and explains the reasoning when something isn't implemented. Collaboration is not consensus — it's permissionless contribution paired with clear ownership.

Sub-values

Kindness
Caring for others creates the framework for challenging directly. "Evaluate accurately, kindly" — they explicitly disagree with Netflix's "accurately, not kindly".
No ego
Don't defend a point to win, don't double down on a mistake. Separate identity from output. Be willing to say "I was wrong."
Assume positive intent
Counter the Fundamental Attribution Error. Read messages charitably. If it sounds like a slight, ask for clarification.
Say thanks
Specific, public praise. There's a #thanks Slack channel. Frequent positive feedback creates a climate where critique is heard as a gift.
Say sorry
Apologising rebuilds trust. Don't quietly disengage when you've written something poorly — repair it.
Negative feedback ratio
Aim for a high positive-to-negative feedback ratio. Negative feedback should be in private (1:1, ideally video). Praise is public.
Don't surprise
No surprises in performance reviews. If something needs to change, raise it now, not at the cycle.
It's impossible to know everything
Admitting "I don't know" is expected, not vulnerable. Asking questions strengthens you and the company.
Get to know each other
Coffee chats, Take a Break Calls, Donut bot pairings. Personal rapport enables async trust.
Short toes
It's not possible to step on anyone's toes — contributing to others' domains is welcomed. Counters the slowdown that comes with scale.
Steel-man arguments
Argue against the strongest version of an opposing view, never a straw man. Represent the other side so they feel understood.
Disagree, commit, disagree
Once a decision is made, execute it — even while continuing to argue for change. Decisions are reversible if shown to be wrong.
Interview signal

Watch for STAR stories where you proactively helped someone outside your remit, where you accepted feedback without defensiveness, or where you committed to a decision you didn't fully agree with. The opposite — building empires, hoarding information, or stalling on consensus — is a red flag.

§ 02 / The R in CREDIT📈Results

"Customers and users come first. We aim for ambitious results and we hold ourselves accountable for delivering them."

GitLab is unusual in pairing aggressive ambition with explicit calm — the goal is shipping the smallest valuable thing now and iterating, not waiting for perfect. Results without iteration becomes hero culture; iteration without results becomes drift. The two values are designed to balance each other.

Sub-values

Customer results
Customers and users always come first. Be obsessive about their problems and the value you create for them.
Ambitious
"While we iterate with small changes, we strive for large, ambitious results." Hard targets. Present-day pessimist, long-term optimist.
High-performance
Hire and retain people who consistently deliver. Underperformance is named, not avoided.
Sense of urgency
"At an exponentially-scaling startup, time gained or lost has compounding effects." Speed beats perfection. Most decisions are reversible — make them quickly without seeking approval.
Accept the situation
Rather than wishing things were different, work with what's actually true and move forward.
Be the change
If something needs fixing, fix it — don't wait to be asked. Initiative is rewarded.
Dogfooding
Use GitLab to build GitLab. The first opinion-former on any feature is the team that ships it. ("Customer Zero" in the JD.)
Manager of one
Set your own priorities, drive your own work, ask for help when stuck. Don't wait for direction.
Bias for action
"Delivering fast results requires the fearless acceptance of occasionally making mistakes; our bias for action also allows us to course correct quickly."
Global optimization
Do what is best for the organization as a whole. Don't optimise for your team's goals if it negatively impacts other teams, users, or the company.
Focus & deferred gratification
Maintain motivation through difficulty and complexity. Avoid analysis paralysis. Don't take the slow, quiet path with no risk.
Measure results, not hours
Async-first means outputs matter, not time at desk. No surveillance, no presenteeism.
Self-service and self-learning
Find the answer in the handbook before asking. Document your answer when you find it.
Interview signal

This is the value most aligned with the JD's "diagnose before building" framing. Stories should show: ambitious targets, measurable outcomes (not just activity), shipping fast under ambiguity, and willingness to say "this doesn't need AI" when it doesn't. Cite flow metrics, ROI, adoption — not lines of code.

§ 03 / The E in CREDIT⏱️Efficiency

"We care about working on the right things, not doing more than needed, and not duplicating work."

Efficiency at GitLab is structural, not personal — it's about the systems that let a 2,000+ person all-remote company function. The handbook, async communication, boring solutions, and the manager-of-one model are all efficiency mechanisms. Working harder is not the answer; working in a way that scales without coordination overhead is.

Sub-values

Boring solutions
Choose proven, simple tech over novel. "Boring" ≠ "bad" or "technical debt" — it means low-complexity, low-risk, well-understood.
Faintest pencil > sharpest memory
Document everything: handbook, meeting notes, issues. Reading is faster than asking.
Spend company money like your own
Frugality, but not cheapness. Optimise total cost, not individual line items.
Reduce cycle time
Shorten the loop between idea and result. Friction in the system is the enemy.
Iteration over consensus
Permissionless innovation. Small teams moving fast > large teams aligning slowly.
Async first
Default to writing, not meetings. Synchronous time is the most expensive resource.
Knowledge management
Capture decisions and rationale. The handbook is the long-term memory of the company.
Reuse over reinvent
If a pattern works, document it and propagate. Avoid duplicating effort across teams.
Bias for self-service
Build tools and docs that let people unblock themselves rather than queueing on humans.
Watch out for "Efficiency" being misread as "do more with less" or "lean staffing". GitLab means it as structural efficiency — the architectural choices that make scale possible. Don't pitch yourself as someone who works 70 hours a week.

§ 04 / The D in CREDIT🌐Diversity, Inclusion & Belonging

"We hire globally and encourage hiring in a diverse set of countries. We work to make everyone feel welcome."

D, I, and B are explicitly three values rolled into one because GitLab argues none works without the others — diversity without inclusion produces tokenism; inclusion without belonging is performative. As an all-remote company hiring from 65+ countries, this isn't a slogan: it's an operational requirement.

Sub-values

Hire globally
Roles are remote and time-zone-flexible by default. They run a double referral bonus for underrepresented candidates.
"Culture fit" is a bad excuse
Don't hire by drink-with-them gut feel. Hire on shared values. Cultural diversity > cultural conformity (no brogrammer atmosphere).
Family and friends first
Personal life takes priority over work. Block time on your calendar. Don't apologise for it.
Inclusive language
Use neutral terminology. Avoid jargon and idioms that don't translate. Default to clear, plain language.
Hold each other accountable
Call in (privately) when something offensive is said, intentional or not. Both giving and receiving feedback builds the culture.
Bias awareness
Recognise unconscious bias in hiring, performance reviews, and decision-making. Surface it openly when it shows up.
Team Member Resource Groups (TMRGs)
Voluntary, employee-led groups (Black@GitLab, Women, Pride, API, Caregivers, etc.) with executive sponsors and budgets.
Inclusive meetings
Numbered agenda docs, written discussion captured live, time-zone-aware scheduling, recording defaults.
Pay transparency
Public compensation calculator. No salary negotiation games. Same role, same band, same offer.
Interview signal

Don't reach for token DEI examples. The strongest evidence is structural: how you've designed processes (interviews, reviews, doc reviews, on-call) to be inclusive of timezone, language, neurodivergence, and seniority. The Mannequin or open-source hackathon stories with junior engineers can speak here without forcing it.

§ 05 / The I in CREDIT👣Iteration

"Move fast by shipping the minimum viable change. We value constant improvement by iterating quickly, month after month."

Iteration is GitLab's most distinctive value and the one most often misread. It does not mean "agile sprints". It means: the smallest improvement that adds value, shipped now, regardless of how unglamorous. If a task is too big to deliver in one month, cut scope. The MR (Merge Request) culture inside the product is the operational expression of this value.

Sub-values

Minimum Viable Change (MVC)
Ship the smallest improvement that adds value now. Resist scope creep. The next iteration is one MR away.
Make a small improvement now
Don't wait for the perfect time. Don't bundle. Don't queue improvements behind a redesign.
Reduce cycle time
Faster iterations beat better iterations. The compound interest is in throughput.
Wrong solutions are fixable
Non-existent solutions are not. Ship something, then refine.
Boring solutions
(Yes, also under Efficiency — it sits across both.) Ship the simplest mechanism that solves the problem.
Knowing the long-term goal
Iteration without a destination is drift. You should always know where the small steps are heading.
Permissionless innovation
Don't seek consensus before improving something. If it's reversible, ship it.
Maintenance > new features
Reliability and quality of existing features is prioritised over net-new shiny.
From here to there
Don't compare against an unreachable ideal. Compare against the previous version.
"Wrong solutions can be fixed, but non-existent ones aren't adjustable at all." — GitLab handbook, on iteration
Interview signal

This is where the JD's "deliver working prototypes in days, not months" lives. Stories should show: scoping back to the smallest valuable thing, shipping under time pressure, picking the boring solution over the impressive one, and resisting the urge to over-engineer. The Codex rollout, Kiro pilot, and Copilot PoV are all good iteration evidence — small landings, then scale.

§ 06 / The T in CREDIT👁️Transparency

"Be open about as many things as possible. By making information public, we reduce the threshold to contribution."

Transparency at GitLab is operational, not aspirational. The handbook is public, issue trackers are public, internal meetings stream to YouTube, financial guidance is shared. Default-public is the rule; private is the exception, and the exceptions are governed by the SAFE framework.

Sub-values

Public by default
Anything not bound by SAFE constraints starts public. Slack channels, GitLab issues, handbook pages — all open unless there's a reason.
Handbook-first
If a process isn't in the handbook, it doesn't exist. Decisions made in Slack don't count until committed to the handbook.
Direct
Say what you mean, mean what you say. No hedging, no political language. Direct is kind.
Findable
It's not enough that the information exists — it has to be retrievable. Good titles, good cross-links, good structure.
Embrace mistakes
Blameless problem-solving. Mistakes are data, not blame surface.
No public complaints
Couple every observation with a question and a suggested action. Don't air grievances without naming the group that can change it.
Disagree by opening an MR
If you disagree with a handbook page, propose the change rather than complaining about it.
Don't use rank
Never invoke a senior person's name to win an argument. Decisions are made on merit, not authority.
Low-context communication
Provide all context up front; assume your reader knows nothing. Designed for global, async audiences.
SAFE framework
The exception list — see below. Only four categories of information stay private.

The SAFE framework — what's not public

SAFE is GitLab's filter for information that should remain internal, and sits alongside a separate Confidentiality Levels page that classifies information as public, internal, or limited access. The default is public; SAFE governs the exceptions.

§ 07 / VocabularyCross-cutting concepts

These show up across multiple values and in nearly every interview. Knowing the term is table stakes; using it correctly is the differentiator.

DRI — Directly Responsible Individual
Operational

Every decision and every piece of work has exactly one DRI. The DRI is the person who decides — they take input from collaborators but don't owe consensus. Disagreements are escalated through respectful conversation, not by overriding the DRI.

Why it matters: It's how GitLab keeps moving despite default-public collaboration. Without DRIs, public input would lead to paralysis.

Manager of One
Operational

The expectation that every team member sets their own priorities, drives their own work, identifies blockers, and asks for help when stuck — without being told. Especially critical in async, all-remote work where no one is watching the clock.

Why it matters: The Senior AI Engineer JD literally says "End-to-End Ownership... comfortable operating with ambiguity and driving to measurable outcomes independently." That's manager-of-one in plain English.

Short toes
Cultural

The idea that it's not possible to step on anyone's toes at GitLab — others contributing to your domain is welcomed, not territorial. Stems from anecdotes where the CEO's own merge request was closed after junior pushback.

Why it matters: Counters the velocity drag that comes with company growth. The opposite of "stay in your lane".

Assume positive intent
Cultural

Counteracts the Fundamental Attribution Error — the tendency to blame circumstances for our own mistakes but blame character for others'. When a message reads as a slight, the default assumption is misunderstanding, not malice. Combined with steel-manning to keep disagreements productive.

Steel-manning
Cultural

The opposite of straw-manning. When disagreeing, articulate the strongest possible version of the opposing view — strong enough that the other side feels accurately represented. Then engage with that version.

Disagree, commit, disagree
Cultural

A modification of the Bezos "disagree and commit". Once a decision is made, you execute it as if it were yours — but you can keep arguing for change while doing so. Every decision is reversible if shown to be wrong.

Permissionless innovation
Operational

You don't need approval to make an improvement. You don't need to ask everyone affected before changing something — they can review the MR after. Default to action; let process catch up.

Handbook-first
Operational

The handbook is the company's single source of truth. New processes, decisions, and changes go into the handbook before they take effect. "If it's not in the handbook, it doesn't exist." Currently >2,000 pages, all public.

Low-context communication
Operational

Always assume your reader has zero context. Provide background, link sources, define terms. Designed for an asynchronous, multi-cultural, multi-timezone audience where you can't clarify in real time.

Async-first
Operational

Default to writing over meetings. Synchronous time is the most expensive resource in an all-remote company. Documents with comments > calls; calls only when async has been tried and failed.

Dogfooding / "Customer Zero"
JD-relevant

Use GitLab to build GitLab. Every feature is used internally before — and after — it ships. The Senior AI Engineer JD calls this out by name: "Be Customer Zero: leverage and showcase GitLab's AI offerings wherever possible, feeding real-world usage insights back to R&D."

Boring solutions
Operational

Choose well-understood, low-complexity tech over novel. "Boring" is not pejorative — it means proven, reliable, easy to onboard onto. Velocity at scale comes from constraining the variety of tools, not maximising it.

Blameless problem-solving
Cultural

When something goes wrong, the question is "what in the system allowed this?" not "whose fault was this?". Blame surfaces are minimised so people surface mistakes faster. Adopted from SRE culture.

No ego / Identity ≠ output
Cultural

Your work is not you. Feedback on your work is not feedback on your worth. This is what makes short toes and direct feedback survivable at scale.

Likely values-fit questions and how to read them

"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision"

What they're testing: disagree-commit-disagree, no ego, transparency.

The trap: stories where you were right and they were wrong, told smugly. Or stories where you went silent.

The win: a story where you raised the disagreement clearly, committed to the decision once made, and either changed your mind on evidence or successfully reopened the conversation later with new data.

"How would you decide between building and not building?"

What they're testing: the JD's "this doesn't need AI" provocation. Diagnostic rigour.

The trap: jumping to a technical framework (RICE, ICE) without first naming the constraint.

The win: walk through value-stream mapping or Theory of Constraints language. Identify the bottleneck before proposing a tool. Mention saying no as a real outcome.

"How do you work async / handle remote / collaborate across time zones?"

What they're testing: low-context communication, handbook-first instinct, async maturity.

The trap: "I have lots of meetings" — the wrong answer for GitLab.

The win: documents-with-comments over calls, default-to-writing, recording yourself if a sync is needed, leaving decisions in retrievable places, low-context updates.

"Tell me about a time you shipped something fast"

What they're testing: MVC, iteration, bias for action.

The trap: heroic 90-hour-week story. They don't want hero culture.

The win: a story about cutting scope dramatically to ship, then iterating. The Codex pilot and Kiro PoV both fit. Lead with the scope decision, not the technical implementation.

"How do you handle a colleague who's underperforming or wrong?"

What they're testing: kindness, directness, no surprises, blameless problem-solving.

The trap: avoidance, or harshness presented as honesty.

The win: private and direct first, focused on the work not the person, with a proposed path forward. If escalation was needed, framed as systemic not personal.

One thing to watch Resist any framing that positions GitHub as the natural alternative to GitLab. Don't bring up the GitHub interview unless asked, and if asked, frame it as "I explored a customer-facing role and decided AI engineering was the right next step" — not as a rejection. GitLab and GitHub competing for talent is a known dynamic; keeping the conversation pointed at your fit for this role is cleaner.

§ 09 / Quick referenceThe TL;DR card

If you can recite this in your sleep, you're prepared.

The acronym

Collaboration · Results · Efficiency · Diversity, Inclusion & Belonging · Iteration · Transparency. The credit team members give each other by assuming positive intent.

The hiring stance

"Culture fit is a bad excuse." They hire on values, not vibes. They want cultural diversity and value alignment, not cultural conformity.

The decision-making model

Every decision has a DRI. Anyone can input. The DRI decides. Decisions are reversible by default. Disagree, commit, disagree.

The default communication mode

Handbook-first, async-first, low-context, public-by-default. Slack is for ephemeral things. The handbook is the long-term memory.

The information default

Public unless filtered out by SAFE: Sensitive, Accurate (not yet verified), Financial (MNPI), Effect (negative impact).

The work default

Manager of one. Set your own priorities, drive your own work, ask when stuck. Output matters, not hours.

The shipping default

Minimum Viable Change. Smallest improvement that adds value, shipped now. Iterate monthly. Wrong solutions are fixable; non-existent ones aren't.

The disagreement default

Steel-man the opposing view. Assume positive intent. Direct + kind. Short toes — your domain isn't a fortress. No ego — your identity isn't your output.
