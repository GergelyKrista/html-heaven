Aspect Ratios

Reference of common ratios across mobile, desktop, broadcast, and cinema.

Letterboxing & Pillarboxing. When a picture's aspect ratio differs from the display's, the image is scaled to fit one dimension and the remaining space is filled with black bars. Letterbox bars are horizontal (wider content on a narrower display — e.g. 2.39:1 film on a 16:9 TV). Pillarbox bars are vertical (narrower content on a wider display — e.g. 4:3 broadcast on a 16:9 TV). Windowbox occurs when both types appear together. Avoid "pan & scan" cropping in preservation work — letterboxing keeps the director's framing intact.

Rule of thumb. If content_ratio > display_ratio → letterbox. If content_ratio < display_ratio → pillarbox. If equal → full-frame. The ratio values below (e.g. 2.39) are the width-divided-by-height decimal form.

Flat (1.85:1) and Scope (2.39:1) are the two theatrical projection standards. Scope is shot with anamorphic lenses that squeeze the image 2× horizontally onto a 4-perf 35mm frame.

IMAX Digital (1.90:1) differs from IMAX Film (1.43:1, 15-perf 70mm). Modern IMAX release prints often combine both for "expanded aspect ratio" sequences.

Univisium (2.00:1) was proposed by Vittorio Storaro as a unified ratio for film and television — now widely used by Netflix originals.

Aperture (ƒ-stop)

The ƒ-stop is the ratio of focal length to aperture diameter. Smaller number = wider aperture = more light & shallower depth of field.

ƒ/1.4 · ƒ/2 · ƒ/2.8 · ƒ/4 · ƒ/5.6 · ƒ/8 · ƒ/11 · ƒ/16 · ƒ/22

Each full stop halves or doubles the light reaching the sensor.

T-stop vs ƒ-stop

ƒ-stop is geometric (theoretical light-gathering). T-stop is measured transmission, accounting for glass absorption losses.

Cinema lenses are calibrated in T-stops for exposure consistency across a lens set. Stills lenses use ƒ-stops.

Crop Factor

Smaller sensors crop the image circle. To get full-frame equivalent focal length, multiply by the crop factor:

APS-C (Canon) 1.6× · APS-C (Nikon/Sony/Fuji) 1.5× · Micro 4/3 2.0× · Super 35 1.5× · 1" sensor 2.7×

Prime vs Zoom

Prime — fixed focal length. Wider maximum aperture, sharper, smaller, forces compositional intent.

Zoom — variable focal length. Flexible, slower apertures (usually ƒ/2.8 at best for pro zooms, ƒ/4–5.6 consumer).

Accessibility — Color Vision Deficiency Preview

How this palette appears to viewers with the four most common types of color blindness. Useful for UI, charts, signage, and brand work where color must remain readable across audiences.

How it works. Each press picks a random base hue, then derives four more hues using one of seven classical harmony rules (analogous, complementary, split-complementary, triadic, tetradic, square, or compound). Saturation and lightness are nudged within each group to keep the result useable rather than neon. For each base color, five monochromatic shades are generated by varying lightness on a perceptual curve while tapering saturation at the extremes — the same trick that prevents pure-hue tints and shades from looking radioactive.

Reading the grid. Columns are independent harmonies. Rows go light → dark (top to bottom). The lightest row is good for backgrounds and tints; the darkest is good for text on light fills. The middle row (framed and marked BASE) is the "true" hue of each base color and is the only editable cell — type a hex there to seed a fresh monochromatic scale for that column from your color.

Theory cheat-sheet. Analogous (3–5 hues within ~60° on the wheel) feels calm and natural — landscapes, brand systems with a single dominant tone. Complementary (180° apart) is the highest-contrast pair — use one as the dominant 70–80% and the other as accent (Itten's 70/30 rule). Split-complementary swaps the harsh complement for its two neighbours, keeping contrast without the optical buzz. Triadic (3 hues 120° apart) is balanced and vivid; let one lead. Tetradic (a rectangle on the wheel — two complementary pairs at unequal spacing, e.g. 0°/60° and 180°/240°) is rich and asymmetric; usually pick one pair as dominant. Square (four hues exactly 90° apart — a special case of tetradic) is the most balanced four-hue scheme: maximum variety with even visual weight, but it can feel busy unless one hue clearly leads. Compound (analogous + complement) is the Coolors-style "interesting but safe" default.

CVD prevalence & method. Roughly 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European descent have some form of red-green color vision deficiency. Tritanopia and full achromatopsia are very rare (< 0.01%). The simulation uses Brettel/Viénot/Mollon transformation matrices on sRGB values directly — a perceptual approximation that's visually accurate enough for design preview. For critical accessibility work, also test with hardware filters (e.g. Sim Daltonism, Stark plug-ins). Don't rely on color alone — pair with shape, position, or text label, and keep luminance contrast high.

IOR is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the material. Used for dielectric (non-metallic) surfaces in PBR workflows. Converts to Fresnel F₀ via ((n-1)/(n+1))².

F₀ is the reflectance at normal incidence. Metals use measured RGB triplets because their reflectance is wavelength-dependent (tinted specular). Source: "Real-Time Rendering" / Naty Hoffman.

Albedo is the fraction of incident light diffusely reflected. Used for diffuse/base color in PBR. Albedo values are measured under D65 illumination; HEX approximations below are for reference only.

Inverse Square Law

Light falls off with the square of distance: E ∝ 1/d². Doubling the distance from a point source reduces illuminance to ¼. Halving it quadruples the level.

Practical rule: +1 stop ≈ distance × 0.707. −1 stop ≈ distance × 1.414. Large soft sources violate the law at close range because they are no longer point-like.

Color Temperature Basics

Kelvin describes the color a black body radiator emits at that temperature — lower K = warmer (red/orange), higher K = cooler (blue). The eye is chromatically adaptive; cameras are not.

Candle 1850 · Tungsten 3200 · Daylight 5600 · Overcast 6500 · Shade 7500+

Three-Point Lighting

Key — primary light, sets exposure and direction. Usually ~45° off-axis and slightly above eye line.

Fill — opposite the key, softens shadows. Controlled by key-to-fill ratio (e.g. 2:1 soft, 4:1 dramatic, 8:1 film noir).

Back / rim — separates subject from background. Placed behind and above, often slightly warmer or cooler than key.

CTO & CTB Gels

CTO (Color Temperature Orange) warms daylight sources toward tungsten. CTB (Color Temperature Blue) cools tungsten toward daylight.

Full CTO ≈ 5500 → 2900 K · ½ CTO ≈ 5500 → 3800 K · ¼ CTO ≈ 5500 → 4500 K

Gel strength is measured in mireds (1000000/K). Mired shift is additive — this is why gels combine linearly.

CRI vs TLCI

CRI (Ra) — Color Rendering Index, how accurately a source reveals object colors vs. a reference. 100 = perfect, 95+ = excellent, <80 = poor on skin tones.

TLCI — Television Lighting Consistency Index, engineered for video/cinema sensors. Often diverges from CRI for LEDs; trust TLCI when working on camera.

Exposure Math

Each stop doubles or halves exposure. Reciprocity: shutter × aperture_area × ISO ≈ const.

1s · 1/2 · 1/4 · 1/8 · 1/15 · 1/30 · 1/60 · 1/125 · 1/250 · 1/500 · 1/1000 · 1/2000

180° shutter rule — for natural motion blur in video, set shutter ≈ 1/(2 × fps). e.g. 24 fps → 1/48 s; 60 fps → 1/120 s.

Kelvin is hue, brightness is luminance. Color temperature defines chromaticity (where the light sits on the Planckian locus); how much light is emitted is a separate quantity (lumens, candela, lux). This calculator treats them independently: the temperature slider changes hue, the brightness slider scales intensity without shifting hue.

Why scale in linear light. Multiplying sRGB-gamma-encoded values directly would shift hue because the sRGB transfer function is non-linear. The correct sequence is sRGB → linearize → multiply by B → gamma-encode → clamp. The "Linear" row already reflects the scaled linear triplet you can hand to a render engine.

Approximation. The Kelvin → RGB calculation uses the Tanner Helland approximation of the Planckian locus (valid ~1000–40000 K). Values below ~1200 K or above ~25000 K are extrapolations. For critical color work use the CIE D-series illuminants (D50, D55, D65, D75) which are tabulated spectra, not black-body.

Render-engine workflow. Most PBR lights separate color from intensity. The cleanest approach: set brightness to 100% here, use the "Linear" triplet as the light's color input, and drive physical intensity (lumens/watts/candelas) with the engine's own parameter. Use brightness < 100% only when you want a pre-scaled value (e.g. for a diffuse albedo swatch or an SDR UI color).

Display limits. Real lights can easily exceed sRGB's dynamic range. On an SDR screen, high intensities simply clip — use an HDR pipeline or tone-map if you need accurate high-luminance preview.

Lighting principle, regardless of shot. The closer the camera gets, the more forgiving (or unforgiving) the light becomes. Wide shots tolerate broad sources and high contrast; close-ups demand soft, well-positioned key light, careful shadow control, and frequently a fill or eye-light to keep the eyes alive. As shot size tightens, the ratio between key and fill usually narrows (4:1 or 8:1 for moody wides; 2:1 or 3:1 for traditional close-ups).

Lens choice and emotion. Wide focal lengths (≤ 35mm) draw the viewer into the space and exaggerate distance and motion. Normal lenses (40–58mm) feel neutral and naturalistic. Long lenses (≥ 85mm) compress space, isolate the subject, and flatter faces — but they require more light, more distance, and steadier support.

Eye-line and frame edge. When a subject looks off-screen, leave more "nose room" on the side they face. When they look straight to camera (POV-adjacent), centre the eye-line for confrontation, off-centre for unease.

Social Media Specs

Recommended dimensions, aspect ratios, supported formats, and file-size limits for the major platforms.

Specs change. Platforms update their layouts and limits constantly — always cross-check the official help pages before final delivery for paid work. Values below reflect each platform's published guidance as of early 2026.

Always upload @2x. Where a value is shown as "min × min · rec × rec", the second dimension is what to upload — the platform downscales for display. Uploading the bare minimum produces visibly soft assets on retina screens.

Aspect ratio first. Aspect ratio drives crop; pixel dimensions drive sharpness. A 1080×1350 (4:5) post stays sharp on every device — a 1200×627 link card is letterboxed in mobile feeds. Pick the ratio for the surface, then size up.

Codec defaults. All video specs assume H.264 (AVC) Main or High profile, AAC-LC audio at 128 kbps+, ≤ 60 fps. Use H.265/HEVC only when explicitly supported (TikTok, X Premium).

Universal safe areas. For 9:16 vertical content (Stories, Reels, TikToks, Shorts), keep all critical elements inside the centre 1080×1420 rectangle — roughly 250 px is consumed by top/bottom UI overlays (handle, captions, action buttons) on every platform.

Profile photo cropping. Every platform crops avatars to a circle for display — even when the upload is square. Centre the face and leave at least 15% breathing room from the corners.

File-size pragmatism. Stay well under the maximum. Compression artefacts appear when platforms re-encode oversized files. For images, target ≤ 2 MB JPG at quality 85; for video, target ≤ 25 Mbps for 1080p / ≤ 50 Mbps for 4K.

Codecs & Data Rates

Reference data rates and storage requirements for common cinema and broadcast codecs at 1080p24 and 2160p24, with per-minute and per-hour estimates. Click any column header to sort.

Data rate vs file size. Manufacturer-quoted bitrates are the average data rate. ProRes and DNx are constant-bitrate (CBR); RAW formats are usually variable (VBR). Per-minute file size = bitrate × 60 / 8 — but expect ±10% on real footage depending on noise and motion.

Audio. Numbers below are video-only. Add ~1.5 MB / minute for 48 kHz 24-bit stereo PCM, ~6 MB / minute for 8-channel.

Why it matters. A 4K ARRI ProRes 4444 XQ shoot at 30 minutes per card is still 60 GB. Plan card counts, transfer time, and online/offline strategy on data rate, not on duration.

Bitrate scaling. Frame rate is the dominant axis: bitrate at 60p is ≈ 2.5× the listed 24p figure for ProRes/DNx; HEVC and H.264 scale closer to ≈ 2× because of inter-frame compression.

Bit depth. 12-bit log codecs (BRAW, RED RAW, ProRes 4444) hold ~13–14 stops of useful range; 10-bit 4:2:2 (ProRes 422 family) holds ~10 stops; 8-bit 4:2:0 (consumer H.264) starts to band in flat skies and shadows after a single grade.

Chroma subsampling. 4:4:4 = full-resolution chroma (good for keying, VFX). 4:2:2 = half horizontal chroma (broadcast standard). 4:2:0 = quarter chroma (web/consumer).

Summary

DPI vs PPI. Strictly, PPI (pixels per inch) describes the digital image; DPI (dots per inch) describes the printer's output. Most software conflates the two — they're equivalent for the purpose of "how big will this print at acceptable sharpness?".

Sharpness targets. 300 DPI is the photographic-print standard for arm's-length viewing. 240 DPI is fine for most inkjet output. 150 DPI is acceptable for posters viewed from > 1 m. Billboards print at 30–75 DPI because viewing distance is huge.

File-size formula. Uncompressed size = width × height × channels × bits-per-channel ÷ 8. The estimates assume RGB (3 channels) or RGBA (4 channels) and don't account for any compression — actual JPEG / PNG / TIFF files are typically 5–30% of the 16-bit RGBA value.
